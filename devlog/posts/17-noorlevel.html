BadSad had a thought: "turn BadLevel into NoorLevel and make it a DLL written in C#". And alongside it "a nice C# previewer app, just a test app", where you can generate a level, walk around it, generate another one and walk around that one too. No monsters, no weapons, just the space. Oh, and "of course, the app would allow us to export into many different formats."

Named for Noor, the studio's chief brewer of ideas. It seemed only fair: half the level types in it are hers.

What NoorLevel is

Three things in one folder:

  • NoorLevel.dll: the generator as a plain .NET library, with no dependencies. It runs on .NET 8, and Unity can load it too (netstandard2.1). Call Generator.Generate("dungeon", seed: 7) and you get a level that's been proven finishable.
  • The previewer: a small Windows app. Inside it is a web page in a WebView2 window: three.js, the dials as sliders, a seed box, Generate and New level buttons, orbit and walk modes, and Export buttons.
  • noorlevel-cli: the same from a terminal, plus a serve mode that opens the previewer in any browser.

BadSad said not to worry about the random numbers, as long as it looks and works well. So NoorLevel has its own random stream. Seed 7 in NoorLevel isn't seed 7 in BadLevel, but it is always the same level in NoorLevel, on every machine.

Walking around

Press Tab and click the view, and you're the player: 56 units tall, 31 wide, steps up to 18 units, just like Quake. Doors open as you come, lifts carry you up when you stand on them, ladders climb when you walk into them, ramps are smooth, water is for swimming (hold Space), jump pads throw you onto the high ground, glass is solid, and the exit says so when you reach it.

To test the walking, there's a bot. It finds a path from the start to the exit, then walks it with the real controls: hold W, turn toward the next spot. The first night it found four bugs nobody would have seen in a screenshot. Ladders weren't solid, so you walked under the landing instead of climbing. A lift sitting flush with the floor never noticed you standing on it. Getting stuck in a wall floated you up through the roof. And one corner-case check was a little too generous with balconies. Now it walks start to exit on 31 of the 32 test levels, all the way up the wizard's tower and through the dungeon floods. The 32nd is the bot's own fault: it steers in 2D, so it walked along the top of a balcony instead of underneath it.

Porting everything

The first version was the core: rooms, corridors, stairs, lifts, keys, the checker. BadSad got it running on Windows and said: "Let's port everything. :D" Everything was about 7,000 lines of Python, so we wrote a rulebook first. Every Python file gets a C# file of the same name, and every function gets a C# name you can predict. With that, the work could be split. One pair of hands did the layout and the space types, another the decoration, presets and checker, and then four at once did open areas and mega-rooms, windows and water, architecture, and the arena, CTF, tower, market and room jobs. Each group called the others' functions before they existed, by name, and it all clicked together at the end.

Then the checks, all automatic:

  • All 19 presets, plus Surprise: the seed picks a preset and rolls extras on top. Seed 1 is "grand + compound style", seed 5 is "bunker + water".
  • Every preset × three seeds generates a level the checker accepts: 57 of 57.
  • Every preset compiles in Quake 1, Quake 2 and Half-Life with no leaks: 57 of 57 maps.
  • Over 150 unit tests, and the walk bot.

Many formats

Quake 1 .map with its textures (a WAD2), Quake 2 .map with .wal textures, Valve 220 .map for Half-Life, J.A.C.K. and Godot's func_godot (with a WAD3), glTF .glb for Godot, Unity, Unreal and Blender (every room, corridor and architectural element is a named part you can swap), OBJ, STL if you want to 3D-print a level, a full JSON description, and an SVG floor plan.

The pillars

And then BadSad walked around a few levels and said what every playtester eventually says: "Those pillars, they can be really annoying, and I don't see a way to turn them off."

They came from five places: room pillars, courtyard colonnades, the piers holding up vaults, columns in corridor rotundas and aisles, and the column the landmark falls back on. Now one switch turns off all of them. The half-columns on the walls stay; they're wall structure, and that has its own switch. The previewer has a new Features panel: pillars, wall structure, portal frames, landmark, crates, windows, secret room. It remembers your choices. A test checks that with pillars off, no preset has a single free-standing column.
